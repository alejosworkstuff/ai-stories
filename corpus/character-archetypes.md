# Character Archetypes

## Archetypes are starting points, not cages

Archetypes give readers a fast handle on a character; depth comes from how the
character deviates from the type. Use the archetype to set expectations, then
complicate it.

## Common archetypes

- The Hero: driven by a want and a deeper need; grows by confronting a flaw.
- The Mentor: provides guidance or a gift, then steps aside; often has a past
  failure that informs the advice.
- The Shadow: embodies what the hero could become; the most compelling
  antagonists believe they are right.
- The Trickster: disrupts the status quo, provides levity, and exposes truths
  others avoid.
- The Threshold Guardian: tests the protagonist's commitment at the boundary of
  the new world.

## Want versus need

A character's conscious want (the external goal) should sit in tension with their
unconscious need (the internal lesson). The arc resolves when pursuing the want
forces them to confront the need. Flat characters usually lack this tension.

## Flaws, wounds, and lies

Memorable characters carry a wound (a formative hurt), a lie they believe because
of it, and a flaw that the lie produces. The story pressures the lie until the
character either embraces the truth (positive arc) or clings to the lie
(tragic arc).

## Agency

Protagonists should make decisions that drive the plot, not merely react. Even in
constrained situations, meaningful choices — what to sacrifice, whom to trust —
keep a character active and the reader invested.
