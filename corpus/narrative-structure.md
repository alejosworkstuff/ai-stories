# Narrative Structure

## Three-act structure

Most satisfying short stories move through three movements. The first act
establishes the protagonist, their ordinary world, and the inciting incident
that disturbs it. The second act raises stakes through escalating complications
and a midpoint reversal where the character's goal or understanding shifts. The
third act delivers the climax — the moment of highest tension — followed by a
brief resolution that shows how the world has changed.

## The story spine

A reliable spine: "Once there was ... Every day ... Until one day ... Because of
that ... Until finally ... And ever since then ...". Each clause forces cause
and effect, which keeps a story from becoming a list of events. Readers feel
momentum when every beat is *caused* by the previous one rather than merely
following it.

## Scenes and sequels

A scene has a goal, a conflict, and a disaster (an outcome worse or more
complicated than intended). A sequel is the quieter aftermath: reaction,
dilemma, and decision that launches the next scene. Alternating scene and sequel
controls pacing — too many scenes exhausts the reader, too many sequels stalls.

## Try/fail cycles

Before a character succeeds, let them fail in instructive ways. A "yes, but" or
"no, and" outcome keeps tension alive: they get what they wanted but it creates a
new problem, or they fail and things get worse. Two or three try/fail cycles
before the climax make the eventual resolution feel earned.

## Endings

Strong endings echo the opening image with new meaning, answer the dramatic
question raised by the inciting incident, and resolve the emotional arc even when
the plot stays ambiguous. Avoid introducing new elements in the final beat;
instead, recontextualize something already present.
